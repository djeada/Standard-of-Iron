## Cold open

[ep07_p01] The second of August, 216 BC. Eighty-six thousand Romans have marched onto this plain. Before the sun sets, most of them will be dead.

## The road

[ep07_p02] Two years earlier, Hannibal Barca left New Carthage in Spain with an army and an oath. He crossed the Pyrenees, the Rhône and the Alps, and came down into Italy with a fraction of the men he set out with.

[ep07_p03] At the Trebia and at Lake Trasimene he destroyed the armies sent to stop him. Rome answered the only way it knew. It raised more men. For the summer of 216 the Senate fielded eight legions, with as many allies again: the largest army the Republic had ever put into the field.

## Armies and commanders

[ep07_p04] It was led by the two consuls of the year. Lucius Aemilius Paullus was a veteran, cautious, and wary of Hannibal. Gaius Terentius Varro had promised the people a quick end to the war. By custom, they commanded on alternate days.

[ep07_p05] Polybius puts the Romans at eighty thousand foot and more than six thousand horse. Hannibal had perhaps forty thousand infantry and ten thousand cavalry: Africans, Iberians, Gauls and Numidians, far from home and held together by one man. Rome had the numbers. Hannibal had a plan.

## The ground

[ep07_p06] Early that summer Hannibal seized the Roman supply depot at Cannae, by the river Aufidus in Apulia. The plain beside it was flat and open, good ground for horsemen. On the second of August the command was Varro's, and he offered battle. For days the two armies had watched each other across the river.

## The battle

[ep07_p07] Varro packed his legions deeper than usual, to break the enemy centre by sheer weight. Hannibal did the opposite. He pushed his Gauls and Iberians forward in a thin, bulging crescent, and held his African veterans back on either wing. At the centre of the crescent stood Hannibal himself, with his brother Mago.

[ep07_p08] By the river, Hasdrubal's Iberian and Gallic horsemen crashed into the Roman cavalry. There was no room to manoeuvre. Men dismounted and fought on foot, and the Roman horse was cut to pieces. Paullus, wounded early by a sling stone, rode across to fight with the infantry.

[ep07_p09] Then the legions advanced. The crescent gave ground, slowly, as Hannibal intended. It flattened, then bent backwards, and the Romans pressed into the hollow, sure that the centre was breaking.

[ep07_p10] It was not. On both sides the African infantry turned inward and fell on the Roman flanks. And Hasdrubal, having routed one wing of horse, rode behind the whole Roman army to scatter the other, then turned and charged into its rear.

## Climax

[ep07_p11] The ring was closed.

## Aftermath

[ep07_p12] Polybius counts seventy thousand Romans killed and ten thousand taken prisoner; Livy, about forty-eight thousand killed. The consul Paullus died on the field, with some eighty men of senatorial rank. Hannibal lost fewer than six thousand, most of them Gauls of the centre.

[ep07_p13] Varro escaped with a handful of horsemen. Rome did not ask for terms. Across southern Italy cities began to go over to Hannibal, Capua among them. The Senate limited public mourning to thirty days and refused to ransom the prisoners.

[ep07_p14] His cavalry commander Maharbal is said to have told him: you know how to win a victory, Hannibal, but not how to use one. The war was not over. It would last another fourteen years.
